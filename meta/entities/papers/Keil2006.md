# Explanation and Understanding

- **Authors:** Frank C. Keil
- **Year:** 2006
- **Journal:** Annual Review of Psychology
- **Volume/Pages:** 57: 227–254
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.57.102904.190100
- **BibTeX key:** Keil2006
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

Keil surveys the cognitive science of explanation and explanatory understanding across four
dimensions: the varieties of explanation (causal patterns, stances, domains), the intrinsic
limits of explanation, the "illusion of explanatory depth" (IOED), and the distributed and
developmental nature of explanatory competence. The central finding with the most direct
toolkit relevance is the IOED: people consistently and substantially overestimate how well
they understand causal mechanisms, rating their understanding far lower after being asked to
provide a detailed explanation [@Keil2006]. This miscalibration is specific to
causal-mechanistic understanding — it does not occur comparably for factual recall,
procedural knowledge, or narrative knowledge. A second key contribution is the concept
of distributed explanatory cognition: agents cope with the inevitable incompleteness of their
own explanatory models by "outsourcing" to expert minds and by maintaining skeletal
causal gists rather than exhaustive mechanistic accounts.

## Methods

Annual review article synthesizing empirical, developmental, and philosophical work on
explanation. Draws on behavioral experiments (explanation-rating paradigms, causal
learning studies, children's "why" questions), Bayesian causal-network models, and
philosophy-of-science frameworks (deductive-nomological models, inference to the best
explanation, coherentism). No original data; representative references span 1947–2005.

## Key Findings

**Explanatory types and structure.** Explanations differ along four dimensions: causal
pattern (common-cause branching, common-effect convergence, linear chain, causal
homeostasis), explanatory stance (mechanical, design/teleological, intentional), content
domain (physics, biology, psychology, etc.), and degree of social/moral loading. These
dimensions are not interchangeable; people spontaneously apply different patterns to
different domains from early childhood, and cognitive biases co-vary with pattern type.

**Causal centrality.** Most everyday explanations invoke cause-and-effect structure, and
causal elements dominate mixed-mode explanations. Counterfactual reasoning and Bayesian
causal nets (Glymour 2001; Gopnik & Glymour 2004) are the primary formal tools for
separating causal from merely correlational structure. Diversity of evidence (consilience)
strengthens explanations; features early in causal chains carry disproportionate weight.

**Illusion of explanatory depth (IOED).** People rate their understanding of causal devices
(zippers, cylinder locks, helicopters) as moderate-to-high until asked to provide a detailed
mechanistic explanation or answer a diagnostic question, at which point ratings drop
substantially and consistently. The effect is robust across ages and is not a generic
overconfidence bias: it is specific to causal-mechanistic knowledge, not to factual or
procedural knowledge. Root causes include: (a) function-for-mechanism confusion — people
confuse knowing a high-level functional relation (a carburetor mixes fuel and air) with
understanding the underlying mechanism; (b) unclear end-states for explanatory depth,
making stopping criteria for "I understand" ill-defined; (c) few opportunities to test
understanding by generating a detailed explanation; and (d) confusion between offline
stored representations and online situationally-supported comprehension.

**Dealing with gaps: distributed cognition and outsourcing.** Because exhaustive
mechanistic understanding is cognitively impossible, agents maintain coarse causal gists and
rely on cognitive division of labor — delegating explanatory responsibilities to identifiable
experts. People (including young children) are adept at inferring who knows what: knowing
that someone understands spinning tops licenses inferences about what else they know
[@Keil2006]. Source credibility evaluation tracks motivational conflicts, general competence,
and domain-specific competence, and this tracking emerges early in development.

**Evaluating explanations.** Three key dimensions: circularity (often missed in complex
discourse), relevance (level-of-analysis must match audience and purpose), and coherence
(constraint satisfaction among elements). All three are error-prone; people hold internally
inconsistent belief sets and fail to detect complex circularities.

**Development and culture.** Causal-explanatory abilities appear very early (pre-verbal
infants explore causal puzzles; three-year-olds ask domain-appropriate "why" questions).
Implicit understanding consistently precedes explicit formulation. Cross-cultural differences
are mainly ordering differences in explanatory default hierarchies, not categorical
availability differences.

## Relevance

**IOED and belief calibration (H02).** The IOED is an empirically grounded instance of
the same phenomenon that `hypothesis:0002-rich-evidence-payloads-improve-graph-calibration`
addresses at the toolkit level: agents (human or AI) who lack rich mechanistic evidence
payloads may assign high confidence to propositions they only superficially understand.
Keil's finding that IOED is domain-specific (causal mechanism, not facts/procedures)
implies that richer typed payloads are most urgently needed for mechanistic causal claims —
exactly the class Science's causal-graph layer represents. The function-for-mechanism
confusion suggests a specific evidence-type tag: `functional_relation_only` (no mechanism
specified), which would flag propositions at elevated IOED risk.

**Reason-coded revisiting (H03).** The IOED's structural causes map onto named failure
modes: `function_for_mechanism`, `implicit_only` (understanding not yet explicitly
formulated), `situationally_supported` (understanding depends on artifact being present),
and `outsourced` (relies on unchecked external expertise). These could be specialized
reason codes that flag propositions where the stated confidence may be inflated by
explanatory illusion rather than genuine mechanistic grounding.

**Working model as epistemic artifact (`hypothesis:0007-working-model`).** Keil's observation that all explanations
are necessarily incomplete, that agents work from causal gists rather than exhaustive
models, and that this incompleteness is often invisible, directly motivates the federated
patchwork working model in `hypothesis:0007-working-model`. A patch is exactly a causal
gist: a bounded neighborhood with known incompleteness. The working model's explicit
provenance and uncertainty representation can be understood as mechanisms for making
explanatory gaps visible, counteracting IOED at the knowledge-graph level.

**LLM agents as fallible sources (`q08`).** Keil's distributed-cognition framing applies
directly to LLM agents drafting evidence summaries. An LLM agent that states a
mechanistic causal claim may be expressing a functional-level understanding without
genuine mechanistic depth — a form of IOED in artificial systems. This connects to
`question:0008-llm-agents-as-fallible-sources` and `question:0005-authoring-cost-audit`.

**Agent-tool operations and cognitive division of labor (`q12`).** The "outsourcing"
strategy — maintaining a gist plus a pointer to a trusted expert — maps onto how Science
models agent-delegated evidence steps. `question:0012-agent-tool-kg-operations` asks how
Science should represent agent operations in the knowledge graph; the cognitive-labor
division documented by Keil implies that provenance for outsourced claims should record
not just the source but the depth of the delegating agent's own verification.

## Limitations

The review synthesizes diverse empirical literatures but does not provide a unified formal
model of explanation or IOED. The IOED experiments focus primarily on artifacts and
mechanical devices; generalizability to abstract causal models (the primary domain of
Science) is plausible but not directly tested. The distributed-cognition claims rely heavily
on informal observations and are not yet formalised into a computable model of delegation
and verification. The development section surveys a fast-moving field and some cited
results may have been revised or refined since 2006.

## Model / Tool Availability

Review article; no software artifact. The IOED paradigm (explanation-rating pre/post
explanation generation) is a methodology that could be adapted as an empirical probe of
AI-agent explanatory calibration.
